Market Intelligence: A Working Handbook

Market intelligence is the work of answering a business question about the world outside your company, using evidence you collected on purpose. This report covers the whole discipline: what it is, how the work runs, where the evidence comes from, how to analyse it, what to publish, how to staff it, what the law allows, and what changed once research agents arrived.

Chapter 1

Foundations and scope

Market intelligence is the collection and analysis of information about the world outside a company, done to answer a defined question and support a decision.

A concrete example. A pricing team asks: can we raise list price 8% in January without losing enterprise renewals? Market intelligence is everything that answers it. What competitors charge today. What buyers said in the last twenty lost deals. What input costs did. What a regulator allows. The answer arrives as a judgment with a confidence level attached, not as a folder of links.

Four tests

PartTest
Outside the companyThe subject is a market, buyer, rival, supplier, regulator, or technology. Not your own funnel.
Collected on purposeSomeone wrote down what needed answering before collection started.
AnalysedA person or model integrated the pieces and reached a judgment. A link list is not analysis.
Tied to a decisionA named person will act differently depending on the answer, by a known date.

If a piece of work fails the fourth test, it is monitoring. Monitoring is useful, and it is cheaper than intelligence. Call it what it is.

The neighbouring disciplines

DisciplineLooks atTypical question
Market intelligenceThe whole external environment: demand, buyers, rivals, channels, suppliers, regulation, technologyIs this market worth entering in 2027?
Competitive intelligenceNamed rivals: capabilities, intentions, weak pointsWill rival X move down-market?
Business intelligenceYour own operations and historyWhich segment churned last quarter and why?
Market researchBuyer attitudes and behaviour, measured with primary methodsWould buyers pay 8% more for feature Y?
Strategic foresightPlausible futures over long horizonsWhat breaks our model by 2032?

Two distinctions do most of the work. Direction: business intelligence points inward, everything else points outward. Width: competitive intelligence narrows to named rivals, market intelligence covers the whole board, including rivals who do not exist yet.

What it is not

Not a feed

A stream of competitor news with no judgment attached transfers the analytic burden to the reader, who has less time and less context than you.

Not a dashboard

Dashboards answer what is happening for metrics you already chose. Intelligence answers what it means, including for things nobody instrumented.

Not research alone

Surveys are one collection method among many. A function that only runs surveys is a research function.

Not a library

Collection without analysis turns the analyst into a librarian. This is the most documented failure pattern in the field.

A warning on market-size estimates. Published estimates of the alternative data market for 2025 range from US$4.6bn to US$19.4bn depending on the firm, because each draws the category boundary differently. When estimates differ by four times, treat all of them as unusable for planning and use actual buy-side spend instead.

Choosing scope before you start

  1. Subject. Name it precisely. "Fintech" is not a subject. "Embedded payment processing for European B2B marketplaces" is.
  2. Angle. Entry, defence, pricing, acquisition, partnership, risk, hiring. The angle decides which evidence counts.
  3. Decision. Who decides, what they decide, and when. Write the date.
  4. Budget. Time, money, and curiosity. A two-day answer and a six-week answer are different products, not the same product at different quality.

Skipping step three is the most common and most expensive mistake in the discipline.

Chapter 2

The cycle: from question to decision

1DirectionWhich decision needs help, by when, and what would change it
2PlanningWhich questions to answer, from which sources, at what cost
3CollectionGet the material
4EvaluationGrade the source and grade the content, separately
5AnalysisIntegrate, test explanations, reach a judgment
6ProductionWrite the product for the decider, not for the analyst
7DeliveryPut it in front of the decider before the decision date
8FeedbackRecord what was decided and what actually happened

Stage 1 owns the whole cycle. The quality ceiling is set at direction. No amount of collection rescues a badly framed question.

Stage 8 is not optional. If you never record outcomes, you never learn whether your judgments are any good. Most corporate programs skip it.

Key intelligence topics and questions

A Key Intelligence Topic (KIT) is a broad area leadership needs covered, tied to a decision or a risk. A Key Intelligence Question (KIQ) is one specific, answerable question underneath it. Jan Herring's original framing sorts KITs into three kinds, and the sort matters because each needs different collection.

KIT typePurposeExampleCollection style
Strategic decisionsSupport a specific pending decisionShould we enter Brazil in 2027?Project. Deep, time-boxed, ends
Early warningDetect a threat before it landsWill rival X cut price in mid-market?Continuous. Tripwires and indicators
Key playersUnderstand a rival, buyer, supplier, or regulator in the roundHow does rival X actually decide on pricing?Continuous. Profile maintained over time

Writing a good question

BadWhy it failsBetter
What is our competitor up to?Not answerable, no boundaryHas rival X hired mid-market sales staff in DACH since January 2026?
Is the market growing?No definition of market or periodDid EU seats for category Y grow more than 12% in the year to Q2 2026?
Will AI disrupt us?No decision attachedWhich of our top five deal-losses in H1 2026 named an AI-native alternative?

The decision header

Put this at the top of every request, before any work starts.

Decision:      Raise enterprise list price 8% effective 1 January 2027
Decider:       VP Pricing, with CFO sign-off
Decision date: 15 October 2026
Delivery date: 26 September 2026
Would change the decision:
  - Two or more of the top four rivals hold price flat through Q4
  - Renewal cohort survey shows above 20% churn intent at +8%
  - Input cost index falls more than 5% by September

The last block is the most valuable. It defines, in advance, what evidence would flip the answer. It stops the analyst chasing everything, and it stops the decider dismissing the finding after the fact.

Evaluation: grade source and content separately

A reliable source can pass on a false claim. An unreliable source can be right. Grade the two independently, using the Admiralty-style scale: a letter A to F for source reliability, a number 1 to 6 for how credible the specific claim is. A rival's published price list is A1. A reseller's claim about that rival's unpublished discounting is C3 until an unrelated source supports it.

Two sources that share one origin are one source.

The 2026 primary research problem

FindingFigureSource
Raw survey responses containing some form of fraud31%Research Defender, cited by iMotions
Average share of collected data discarded for quality and panel fraud38%, up to 70% for someKantar, cited by iMotions
AI agents passing as human respondentsAn agent evaded every detection method then in useWestwood, PNAS, late 2025, cited

Defences, in order of effect:

  1. Use identity-verified panels when the answer matters. Cost per complete rises. Usable data per euro rises more.
  2. Instrument the survey. Attention checks, completion-time floors, duplicate and geolocation checks, bot scoring.
  3. Treat open-ended answers as suspect. Machine-written open ends are fluent, generic, and increasingly common. Read a sample by hand, always.
  4. Prefer interviews for high-stakes questions. Twenty structured interviews beat a compromised sample of 800.
  5. Report your exclusion rate. If you discarded 38% of responses, say so. It changes how the reader should weigh the result.

Buying alternative data: the checklist

The category reached roughly US$2.8bn in buy-side spend in 2025, across 2,805 tracked datasets. Average clients per dataset fell from 25 to 20, which means supply is growing faster than demand and buyers have leverage on price. Get written answers before you buy.

QuestionWhy it matters
Where does the raw data originate, and who consented?Determines GDPR and privacy exposure
Is any of it non-public information about a specific issuer?Determines MNPI exposure
What is the panel, and how is it scaled to the universe?Determines whether levels or only direction are usable
What is the history, and has the methodology changed?Backfills recomputed under a new method are not a real history
What is the reporting lag and revision policy?Decides whether it can support early warning at all
Coverage by geography, segment, and company size?Most datasets are strong in the US and thin elsewhere
Exclusivity and redistribution terms?Decides whether the edge survives contact with competitors

Ask the vendor for a period where their data was wrong, and what they changed. A vendor with no answer has not looked.

Collection hygiene

  • Date everything. A figure without a period is not evidence.
  • Keep the raw artefact. Screenshot the pricing page, save the filing, archive the job ad.
  • Record the retrieval date alongside the publication date.
  • Never launder a source. If the number came from a vendor blog quoting a survey, cite the blog, not the survey you have not read.
  • Log what you looked for and did not find. Absence of evidence is evidence and it belongs in the record.

Chapter 4

Analysis and judgment

Analysis is the step where observations become a claim about what is true or what will happen, with the uncertainty stated. Everything before it is housekeeping.

Two methods. Use both and reconcile. Lead with bottom-up.

Analysis of competing hypotheses

Start with the full set of plausible explanations, not the favourite one. Build a matrix with hypotheses across the top and evidence down the side. Then try to disprove, not prove. Worked example: rival X drops price 20%.

EvidenceH1: Winning shareH2: Clearing inventoryH3: Cash pressureH4: Blocking our launch
Cut applies only to last-gen SKUInconsistentConsistentNeutralInconsistent
Sales headcount flatInconsistentConsistentConsistentInconsistent

H1 and H4 carry three inconsistencies each. H2 and H3 carry none. The interesting output is not the answer but the discovery that the popular internal story, that they are attacking us, is the worst-supported one.

Saying how sure you are

Two separate things, constantly conflated.

Likelihood

How probable the event is. Almost certainly 95 to 99%. Very likely 80 to 95%. Likely 55 to 80%. Roughly even 45 to 55%. Unlikely 20 to 45%. Very unlikely 5 to 20%. Almost certainly not 1 to 5%.

Confidence

How much you trust the basis. High: multiple independent origins, few assumptions. Moderate: credible sourcing, some gaps. Low: fragmentary or single-origin evidence, heavy assumption load.

Written correctly: "We judge it likely (60% to 70%) that rival X launches a mid-market tier before Q2 2027. Moderate confidence, based on hiring and channel signals from two independent origins, with no direct evidence of pricing."

Calibration

Forecast quality is measurable. The Good Judgment Project scored forecasters with the Brier score, where lower is better. Its findings transfer directly: start from the base rate then adjust; update often in small steps; teams beat individuals; be decisive as well as calibrated, because always saying "roughly even chance" is safe and useless.

The corporate version: write a numeric probability for every material judgment, with a resolution date. Score them quarterly. Within a year you will know which analysts and which source classes to trust.

The one-line test. Before publishing, answer in a sentence: what should the reader do differently, and how sure are we? If you cannot, the analysis is not finished.

Anatomy of a good product

1. Bottom line up front   The judgment, in one or two sentences, with
                          likelihood and confidence
2. So what                What the reader should do differently
3. Key judgments          Three to five, each with likelihood and confidence
4. Evidence               What we saw, dated, with origin count per judgment
5. Alternatives           The next most plausible reading, and why we rejected it

   our mind
7. Sourcing               Source list with grades, and named gaps

Parts 5 and 6 are what separate intelligence from opinion. They are also the first two sections cut under deadline pressure. Do not cut them.

Bottom line up front, done properly

Weak. "This report examines rival X's recent pricing activity and its potential implications for our enterprise segment."

Strong. "Rival X will likely (60% to 70%) launch a mid-market tier before Q2 2027, moderate confidence. If it lands at our current entry price, expect pressure on roughly 18% of our renewal base. Recommend we decide on a defensive tier by 15 October."

Win/loss programs

InterviewsWhat you can say
20 to 30Directional patterns for one segment or matchup
50 or moreSegment by deal size, persona, and vertical
100 or morePrimary loss themes stabilise

Reported outcomes, all self-reported by vendors and practitioners: structured programs are associated with win-rate improvements of 15% to 25% over two years against rep self-reporting alone, and 63% of companies running structured programs report win-rate improvement, rising to 84% for programs running longer than a year. Treat these as a reason to run the program, not a forecast of your result. Nobody publishes the failures.

  1. Interview buyers, not your reps. The rep's account is systematically biased toward price and away from anything the rep controls.
  2. Interview wins too. Losses tell you what went wrong. Wins tell you what to repeat, and they are cheaper to obtain.
  3. Use a neutral interviewer. Buyers soften their answers when the vendor is in the room.

Market maps

A market map is a claim about how a space is organised, disguised as a picture. Publish the inclusion criteria and, more importantly, the exclusion list: the companies you considered and left out, with the reason. That is the part everyone skips and the part that makes the map trustworthy.

Cadence and delivery

The strongest public evidence: 56% of teams share intelligence with sales weekly or faster, and reported revenue impact splits 79% versus 41% between weekly and monthly-or-slower cadences. Vendor survey, self-reported, correlational, and still the best number in the field.

  • Push to the person, not to the portal. The portal is the archive, not the channel.
  • Meet the reader where they work. Sellers live in the CRM and in chat, not in your wiki.
  • Tag by decision, not by source. People search for the question they have.
  • Make the archive findable. A widely quoted Forrester figure holds that over 90% of customer intelligence becomes unfindable within 90 days of collection, not deleted but lost. Whether or not the exact number holds, every practitioner recognises the failure.

Quality bar checklist

  • The judgment appears in the first three sentences
  • Likelihood and confidence are both stated, using the fixed scales
  • Every number carries a period and a source
  • Every key judgment names its origin count
  • The most plausible alternative reading is stated and addressed
  • "What would change our mind" is specific and observable
  • The recommended action names an owner and a date
  • Known gaps are declared, not hidden
  • Nothing in it would embarrass the company if the rival read it

Put it where the decisions are, then give it a standing line to one other function so it does not become captive to a single reader.

Roles

Five jobs: head of intelligence (requirements, the KIT list, the relationship with deciders, the quality bar), analyst (analysis, judgment, products), collection and tooling (sources, feeds, vendor contracts, the archive), field network (structured access to sellers, support, partners, customers), and a part-time compliance sponsor. The most common staffing error is hiring collectors and calling them analysts. Collection is increasingly automatable. Judgment is not.

Chapter 7

Law and ethics

A working map, not legal advice. Rules differ by country and by industry, and several of the cases below are still moving. Get counsel before anything near a line.

How you obtained the information matters more than what the information is. A competitor's manufacturing cost, worked out by buying their product and taking it apart, is lawful nearly everywhere. The same number, obtained by an investigator pretending to be their employee, is not.

Trade secrets

Both major systems landed in a similar place in 2016. In the United States, the Economic Espionage Act of 1996 as amended by the Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016. In the European Union, Directive (EU) 2016/943. Both define a trade secret the same way in substance: information the owner took reasonable steps to keep secret, which has economic value because it is not generally known.

RouteStatus
Reverse engineering a lawfully obtained productExpressly permitted under both the DTSA and the EU Directive
Independent derivationLawful
Observation of what is publicly visibleLawful
Other honest commercial practices (EU) or other lawful means (US)Lawful

Unlawful: acquiring the secret through theft, bribery, misrepresentation, breach or inducement of a duty of confidence, or espionage. Inducing someone else's breach counts. Asking a rival's employee to share confidential material puts you inside the prohibition even though you did not take anything yourself.

The ethics line, drawn by real cases

CaseWhat happenedOutcome
HP pretexting, 2006 HP's investigators impersonated board members and journalists, in some cases using their Social Security numbers, to obtain phone records while hunting a boardroom leak. Both cases share a pattern: the collection was performed by people who believed they were serving the company, under pressure, without a clear written boundary. The fix is a written policy plus a named escalation path, not exhortation.

The SCIP Code of Ethics

The most-cited standard in the field. Reproduced from a secondary source, because scip.org blocks automated retrieval. Verify against the primary page before quoting it formally.

  1. Elevate the profession. Continually strive to increase the recognition and respect of the profession.
  2. Always in compliance. Comply with all applicable laws, domestic and international.
  3. Transparent. Accurately disclose all relevant information, including one's identity and organisation, prior to all interviews.
  4. Conflict-free. Avoid conflicts of interest in fulfilling one's duties.
  5. Honest. Provide honest and realistic recommendations and conclusions.
  6. Act as an ambassador. Promote this code within one's company, with third-party contractors, and within the profession.
  7. Strategically aligned. Adhere to one's company policies, objectives, and guidelines.

Clause 3 is the operative one and the one most often broken. It rules out calling a competitor's support line pretending to be a customer, and attending a rival's webinar under a false name. If your collection method depends on the other party not knowing who you are, stop.

Web scraping

hiQ Labs v LinkedIn narrowed the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act so that it does not reach automated collection of data that is publicly accessible without authentication. Note the counter-example inside the same case: hiQ did get into trouble for hiring contractors to create fake accounts to reach logged-in data.

Meta Platforms v Bright Data. On 23 January 2024, Judge Edward Chen of the Northern District of California granted summary judgment for Bright Data. The reasoning that matters: Meta's terms govern "your use" of its products, and Bright Data did not use Facebook when it scraped public logged-out pages after terminating its accounts.

LineRule
AuthenticationDo not bypass a login. Do not create fake accounts. This is where both defendants got into trouble
Personal dataGDPR and CCPA apply to scraped personal data whether or not it was publicly visible
CopyrightExtract facts. Do not republish creative expression
HarmRespect rate limits. Causing degradation converts a civil argument into a much worse one

GDPR. EDPB Opinion 28/2024 recognised legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f) as a viable basis for AI model development, subject to a rigorous three-part test. EDPB Guidelines 03/2026 on web scraping for generative AI, adopted July 2026, confirmed that GDPR applies whenever scraping involves personal data regardless of public visibility, and that consent is generally not viable at scale. Practical consequence: scraping company pages, prices, and documentation is low risk. Scraping named individuals requires a documented legitimate-interest assessment, data minimisation before collection, and a retention decision. Write the assessment before you collect.

Material non-public information and alternative data

The SEC's Division of Examinations published a risk alert on deficiencies in advisers' use of alternative data and expert networks, with MNPI as the central concern. In the Primary Global Research matter, the SEC charged hedge funds and portfolio managers with trading on MNPI obtained from public-company insiders moonlighting as expert-network consultants, alleging more than US$30 million in illicit profits.

Expert networks were never banned. The channel is not the problem. The control over what flows through it is.

ControlDetail
Pre-clearanceApprove the expert and the topic list before the call

Antitrust: competitor information and pricing

The DOJ announced a proposed settlement with RealPage on 24 November 2025, resolving allegations that its revenue-management product used competing landlords' data in an algorithm producing pricing recommendations. The settlement bars RealPage from using competitors' non-public data in that product and limits model training to historic, backward-looking non-public data at least 12 months old.

The enforcement posture, as read by counsel: the DOJ is not against algorithmic pricing tools as such. It is against tools that pool non-public competitor data and produce coordinated outputs. Risk is highest when the data is current or forward-looking, non-public, competitively sensitive, and used at runtime in a pricing decision. Risk rises further when the recommendation is applied automatically with no human override.

DoDo not
Collect published prices, public rate cards, public tender resultsExchange current or forward-looking non-public pricing with a competitor, directly or through any intermediary
Use aggregated, historic, anonymised benchmarks from a neutral third partyContribute your own forward-looking pricing into a pool that feeds competitors' pricing
Keep a human decision step between any recommendation and a price changeLet a tool trained on rivals' non-public current data set prices automatically
Document the provenance of every input to a pricing modelAssume a vendor's data is clean because the vendor says so

A one-page policy you can enforce

Always allowed

Public sources, published prices, filings, patents, job ads, public documentation, logged-out pages. Buying and testing a competitor's product, including taking it apart. Attending public events and identifying yourself honestly. Interviewing anyone after disclosing who you are. Talking to your own customers, prospects, and partners.

Allowed with controls

Expert-network calls with pre-clearance and a scope script. Purchased alternative data after provenance and MNPI diligence. Scraping public pages within rate limits and with a legitimate-interest assessment where personal data is involved. Research with a competitor's customers where the sponsor is disclosed or the design is cleared by counsel.

Never

Misrepresenting who you are. Fake accounts or bypassing any login. Asking anyone to breach a confidentiality obligation. Taking physical material, including from bins. Recording without the consent local law requires. Trading on non-public information about a specific issuer. Pooling current or forward-looking non-public pricing with competitors.

Escalation. Any grey case goes to a named person before collection, not after. Write the name in the policy.

Chapter 8

What changed by 2026

Research agents made collection and first-draft synthesis fast and cheap. They did not make verification cheap. The bottleneck moved from finding evidence to deciding what is true. Every practical change below follows from that one shift.

AlphaSense launched Deep Research on 10 June 2025, an agent running multi-step research over a corpus of more than 500 million business and financial documents: equity research, earnings calls, expert-call transcripts, filings, news, and internal documents for enterprise customers. It produces primers, screening work, and meeting briefings with citations, in minutes. General-purpose equivalents from the large model providers now do the open-web version of the same job.

The reliability ceiling

Read these as orders of magnitude, not measurements. They come from aggregator sites, benchmarks differ in construction, and results move month to month. The robust conclusion survives the imprecision: an unverified machine-written citation is not evidence.

Synthetic respondents: useful, oversold

A Stanford study in 2024 found agents built from rich real interviews matched a person's own survey answers roughly 85% as well as that person matched their own answers two weeks later. Peer-reviewed work also finds that synthetic respondents collapse variance and can flip the sign of key relationships. Even when explicitly asked for diverse personas, outputs cluster around a narrow stereotypical range.

The industry position is consistent across ESOMAR, GreenBook, Bain, NIQ and Kantar: a supplement that must be validated against real humans, not a replacement. The ICC/ESOMAR Code introduced an official definition of synthetic data in June 2025.

Defensible

Pre-testing a questionnaire. Generating hypotheses to test with real respondents. Rehearsing an objection or a buying-committee conversation. Filling a small gap in an otherwise human sample, with the share disclosed.

Indefensible

Sizing a market, setting a price, or claiming a preference share from synthetic responses alone. Variance collapse means you get a confident answer that is wrong in a predictable direction.

A verification protocol for AI-assisted intelligence

  1. Claim-level sourcing. Every load-bearing claim carries a source and a retrieval date. No source, no claim.
  2. Open the source. Never cite a document the agent found but nobody opened. Machine-generated citations point to real documents that do not always say what the summary claims.
  3. Two independent origins for load-bearing numbers.
  4. Keep the raw quote next to the claim.
  5. Date every figure.
  6. Mark the tier: directly sourced, inferred, or assumed. Readers weigh them differently and deserve the chance to.
  7. Declare AI involvement in the product. Increasingly a compliance matter as well as an honesty one.

What did not change

  • Requirements still decide quality. A fast answer to the wrong question is worse than no answer, because it is more convincing.
  • The legal perimeter is unchanged by speed, and in Europe it tightened.
  • Buyers still tell you things no dataset contains. Twenty good interviews still beat a large corrupted survey.
  • Decisions are still made by people who have nine minutes.

Chapter 9

Build guide: first 90 days

For one person, or a small team, standing up a market intelligence function from nothing. Assumes no platform purchase in the first 90 days. That is deliberate.

Day 0 to 15: decide what you are for

TaskOutput
Interview the five people whose decisions you will serveA list of decisions they face in the next four quarters, with dates
Convert those into KITs and KIQsOne page. Between 4 and 8 KITs. No more
Name your executive sponsorA person, in writing, who will read the output and act
Write the ethics policyOne page, with legal sign-off
Inventory what already existsExisting reports, subscriptions, dashboards, unused CRM fields
Agree the first three products and their cadenceUsually: weekly digest, one battlecard set, one deep dive

The interview script that works:

1. What decision are you making in the next two quarters that you feel
   under-informed about?
2. What would you need to know to feel confident?
3. What is the last thing that surprised you about this market?
4. Where do you go for market information today, and what is wrong with it?
5. If I could put one page on your desk every week, what would be on it?

Question 3 is the most productive. Surprises map directly to the early warning topics you should be covering and are not.

Day 16 to 45: build the evidence base

  • Build the source inventory, ranked by the source ladder
  • Mine what you already own: lost-deal reasons, support tickets, churn notes, call transcripts
  • Set up free monitoring: price-page diffs, job-ad watches, filing alerts, changelog feeds
  • Run 15 to 20 win/loss interviews. This will be your highest-value asset by day 90
  • Grade your sources and write the grades down
  • Ship the first weekly digest, even if thin. Rhythm before richness

Day 46 to 75: publish and close the loop

  • Ship the first battlecard set. Top 3 competitors only, one screen each
  • Ship the first deep dive, answering the highest-value KIT
  • Start the judgment log: every probabilistic judgment, with date and resolution date
  • Set tripwires: the specific observable that triggers an alert, and who receives it
  • Establish the archive, tagged by decision and question, not by source
  • Collect feedback formally: did this change anything you did?

Day 76 to 90: measure and decide what to scale

  • Baseline the four metrics
  • Score the first judgments that have resolved, even three or four
  • Review the KIT list with the sponsor. Kill what nobody used
  • Write the tooling case, now that you know which layer constrains you
  • Decide build versus buy per layer. Buy monitoring, build judgment

The 90-day success test

  • A one-page KIT list agreed with a named sponsor
  • Three products shipping on a fixed cadence
  • At least 15 buyer interviews conducted and coded
  • A judgment log with dated, probabilistic entries
  • At least one tripwire that fired, or was demonstrated to work
  • One decision a named person will say your work changed
  • A written ethics policy with a named escalation contact

If you can point to the sixth item, the program is real. If you cannot, fix requirements and delivery before you buy any tool. No platform has ever converted a program that nobody was waiting on.

Reference

Glossary

No terms match that filter.

ACH (analysis of competing hypotheses)

A method that starts with every plausible explanation, then tries to disprove each against the evidence. The explanation with the least contradicting evidence wins.

Admiralty scale

A two-part grading system. A letter A to F grades the source's reliability. A number 1 to 6 grades how credible the specific claim is. Graded separately, because a reliable source can pass on a false claim.

Alternative data

Data not produced for financial reporting, bought to infer company or market performance: card transactions, phone location data, satellite images, scraped pages, job postings, app telemetry.

Base rate

How often something of this general kind happens. Good forecasters start here and adjust for the specifics.

Battlecard

A one-screen reference a seller uses during a live conversation with a prospect who is also considering a named competitor.

BLUF (bottom line up front)

Writing the conclusion in the first sentences, before the evidence.

Brier score

A measure of how good probabilistic forecasts were. Lower is better, zero is perfect. Rewards both being right and being appropriately confident.

Business intelligence (BI)

Analysis of a company's own internal data to improve its own operations. Points inward.

CFAA

The US anti-hacking statute, narrowed by hiQ v LinkedIn so that it does not cover automated collection of data publicly accessible without a login.

Commercial due diligence

Research before an acquisition or investment to test whether the target's story about its market, customers, and competitive position is true.

Competitive early warning

A system of pre-set indicators that fires when the market moves in a way that threatens the current strategy. Gilad's term for the gap between market reality and company strategy is "industry dissonance".

Competitive intelligence (CI)

Intelligence about named competitors: capabilities, intentions, weaknesses. Narrower than market intelligence.

Confidence

How much you trust the basis of a judgment: sourcing quality, independent origins, assumption load. Distinct from likelihood.

Deep research agent

A system that plans a research task, runs many searches and reads, and produces a cited synthesis. Fast at drafting. Not a substitute for checking.

DTSA

Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016. US federal civil claim for misappropriation. Expressly permits reverse engineering and independent derivation.

EDPB

European Data Protection Board. Issues authoritative GDPR guidance, including Opinion 28/2024 and Guidelines 03/2026 on scraping.

Estimative language

Standardised words for probability, so that "likely" means the same thing to writer and reader.

Expert network

A firm arranging paid calls between clients and practitioners with specific industry experience. High signal, high compliance attention.

Intelligence cycle

The loop from decision need, through collection and analysis, to a delivered product and recorded outcome. A management model, not a literal description of practice.

KIT (key intelligence topic)

A broad area leadership needs covered, tied to a decision or a risk. Sorted into strategic decisions, early warning, and key players.

KIQ (key intelligence question)

One specific, answerable question under a KIT.

Legitimate interest

One of six GDPR legal bases, requiring a documented three-part test. The main available basis for large-scale scraping, since consent is generally not workable at scale.

Likelihood

How probable an event is, stated as a band. Distinct from confidence.

Market intelligence (MI)

Collection and analysis of information about the external environment, done to answer a defined question and support a decision.

Market map

A visual and tabular claim about how a market is organised. Trustworthy only if it publishes both its inclusion criteria and its exclusion list.

Market research

Measurement of buyer attitudes and behaviour, usually through primary methods. One collection method inside market intelligence, not a synonym for it.

Mirror imaging

Assuming a competitor will act as you would, with your incentives. A recurring source of wrong predictions.

MNPI

Material non-public information. Trading on it, or passing it to someone who trades, is unlawful in most markets.

Origin

The ultimate source of a claim, as opposed to the outlet that repeated it. Three articles quoting one press release are one origin.

OSINT

Open source intelligence. Intelligence produced from publicly available information. The backbone of business market intelligence.

PESTEL

A scan across political, economic, social, technological, environmental, and legal forces. Produces a list, not a ranking.

Premortem

Assuming, before committing, that the decision failed badly, and writing the story of how.

Pretexting

Obtaining information by pretending to be someone you are not. Prohibited by the SCIP code and at the centre of the 2006 HP scandal.

Primary research

Evidence you generate yourself: interviews, surveys, observation, testing.

Reverse engineering

Taking apart a lawfully obtained product to learn how it works. Expressly lawful under both the DTSA and EU Directive 2016/943.

SAM

Serviceable available market. The part of the total market you could realistically reach, given your model, product, and geography.

SCIP

The professional body for the field, founded in Washington DC in 1986. Renamed twice, most recently in April 2023 to the Strategic Consortium of Intelligence Professionals.

Scenario planning

Constructing several internally consistent futures and testing strategy against each. Deliberately not a forecast.

Signal

An observable proxy for something you cannot see directly. Job ads as a proxy for expansion. Pricing-page changes as a proxy for strategy.

SOM

Serviceable obtainable market. The part of the SAM you can realistically win in a defined period.

Structured analytic technique

A procedure that forces an analyst to consider what they would otherwise skip. The term entered US intelligence use in 2005.

Synthetic respondent

A model-generated persona answering research questions in place of a person. Useful for pre-testing and hypothesis generation. Not a basis for sizing or pricing, because it collapses variance.

TAM

Total addressable market. Total revenue available if you captured the entire market. Easily inflated. Meaningless without SAM and SOM beneath it.

Trade secret

Information kept secret through reasonable steps, which has economic value because it is not generally known. Defined substantially the same way by US and EU law since 2016.

Triangulation

Confirming a claim from two or more genuinely independent origins, ideally collected by different methods.

Tripwire

A pre-set observable that, when seen, automatically triggers an alert. The mechanism that turns monitoring into early warning.

War game

A structured exercise in which teams play competitors and respond to a proposed move.

Win/loss analysis

Structured interviews with buyers after a deal closes, won or lost, to learn why. The highest-return recurring primary research most B2B companies can run.
